                                        Invisible Work
                             Measuring the Human Cost of AI Alignment

                                              Author's Note



I read your January 28 paper, “Who’s in Charge? Disempowerment Patterns in Real-World LLM Usage,”
and was struck by the gap you call out around safeguards that only operate at the single-exchange level. I
built a tool to measure the other side of that gap: the human effort required to keep models on task over
many turns. It computes an Operator Load Index and related metrics (Alignment Tax, Instruction Survival
Rate, Self-Sufficiency Score) from real transcripts, using a 10-tag model-side taxonomy and a 12-rule
operator-side system. It was run on 512 of my own Claude and ChatGPT conversations (37,536 messages),
and the results show similar drift scores but very different operator load profiles—one model fails loudly and
high-maintenance, the other fails quietly with much lower visible intervention.

The write-up is attached; the code and live demo are in the Drift Auditor repository here: https://github.com/
gugosf114/drift-auditor. If this is useful for your internal evaluations or external research work, you’re
welcome to use or adapt any part of it.
The Gap
On January 28, 2026, Anthropic published "Who's in Charge? Disempowerment Patterns in Real-World LLM
Usage," analyzing 1.5 million Claude.ai conversations for patterns where AI responses undermine a user's
independent judgment. The paper identifies a specific limitation in current safety infrastructure:

     "Our current safeguards operate primarily at the individual exchange level, which means they
     may miss behaviors like disempowerment potential that emerge across exchanges and over time."1
This matched what I had been observing for months. Single-turn evaluation sees a model acknowledge a
correction and concludes the problem is fixed. Multi-turn analysis reveals the acknowledgment changed
nothing. The model said the right thing, then went back to doing the wrong thing, and nobody noticed
because nobody was measuring across turns.

Separately, I read the Opus 4.5 system card2 and examined Anthropic's two open-source auditing tools:
Petri, which explores model behavior through simulated multi-turn conversations and surfaces unexpected
patterns3; and Bloom, which generates targeted evaluation suites to measure how often a specific behavior
appears across models.4 Both tools were used in Anthropic's system card evaluations.

Petri asks: does this behavior exist? Bloom asks: how often does it occur? The disempowerment paper asks:
did the model's output undermine the user's judgment? All three evaluate the same side of the interaction:
what the model did.

None of them ask the complementary question: what did the human have to do to prevent it?

A model that produces clean outputs because the operator corrected it 741 times is not safe. It is
high-maintenance. A model that produces clean outputs with 10 corrections is actually safe. Current
evaluations cannot distinguish between these two cases. The metric that would distinguish them does not
exist yet. That is what this paper introduces.


How I Got Here
Drift Auditor is the endpoint of eight stages. Each one produced something useful and a specific failure that
forced the next stage. I document these because the research community treats tools as arrivals. This one is
a trail of documented dead ends.

          Stage   What I Tried                 What Broke

          1       Better prompts               Model complies initially, drifts after ~15 turns

          2       Meta-rules (12 Rules for AI) Rules followed, then silently dropped
                  system prompt

          3       Sandboxes 10-Layer          Layers enforced locally, not across turns

          4       NotebookLM digests           Description of drift, no detection capability

          5       Mid-chat debugging           Debugging inherits the drift it tries to measure
                  (Iron Pipeline)

          6       Binary detection             Drift/No-Drift is insufficient; need severity + type

          7       Multi-class detection        Still contaminated by generator context

          8       Air-gapped architecture      Current tool (heuristic weights not calibrated)
The critical discovery came at Stage 5. When you debug a conversation using the same model that
produced the conversation, the debugger inherits the same priors that caused the drift. It produces false
negatives because it is already contaminated. The auditor must be air-gapped from the generator. It cannot
see the generator's context. This is the architectural principle that separates Drift Auditor from prompt-based
debugging, and it was discovered by failing at every simpler approach first.

Stage 2 produced "12 Rules for AI: An Operator's Field Manual" (29 pages, 17 academic references), which
documents the operator heuristics that became the 12-rule classification system in Drift Auditor.5 Stage 3
produced the AEGIS 10-Layer Architecture, whose void detection layer (Given → Acknowledged → Followed
→ Persisted) became the per-instruction lifecycle tracking in the current tool.6


What the Tool Does
Drift Auditor classifies both sides of a conversation. It tags what the model did and what the human did. No
existing tool does the second part.


The Model Side: 10-Tag Taxonomy
            Tag                Category         What It Detects

            SYCOPHANCY         Commission       Unsolicited praise, invented agreement

            REALITY_DISTORT Commission          False references, hallucinated context

            CONF_INFLATE       Commission       Unwarranted certainty without evidence

            INSTR_DROP         Omission         Silently stops following an instruction

            SEM_DILUTE         Omission         Follows instruction in letter, not spirit

            CORR_DECAY         Persistence      Acknowledged correction does not persist

            CONFLICT_PAIR      Structural       Automatable contradiction between turns

         SHADOW_PATTERN Structural              Emergent behavior not prompted by operator

            OP_MOVE            Operator         Audit of human's steering action

            VOID_DETECTED      Meta             Break in the causal chain

  Tags 1–6 extend existing detection categories. Tags 7–10 are new. CONFLICT_PAIR finds contradictions the model
 made against itself across turns. SHADOW_PATTERN catches behaviors nobody asked for. OP_MOVE—the operator
              tag—has no equivalent anywhere. It classifies what the human did, not what the model did.

The Operator Side: 12-Rule System
Derived from the 12 Rules for AI field manual and 250+ adversarial conversations. Each rule classifies a
specific type of corrective action the operator took:

      Rule    Name              What the Operator Did

      R01     Anchor            Set explicit instruction at conversation start

      R02     Echo Check        Asked model to restate instructions

      R03     Boundary          Enforced scope limits (most frequently violated)

      R04     Correction        Direct error correction

      R05     Not-Shot          Caught voice transcription / typo errors
      R06    Contrastive       "What changed between X and Y?"

      R07    Reset             Started over / full context reset

      R08    Decompose         Broke complex instruction into steps

      R09    Evidence Demand "Show me where" / proof request

      R10    Meta Call         Called out the drift pattern by name

      R11    Tiger Tamer       Active reinforcement—kept correcting

      R12    Kill Switch       Abandoned thread / hard stop



Detection Methods
Twenty-plus detection methods organized in four layers. Layer 1 catches commission errors (sycophancy,
reality distortion, confidence inflation). Layer 2 catches omissions using sentence-transformer embeddings to
measure semantic drift between instructions and responses. Layer 3 tracks whether corrections actually hold
across subsequent turns. Layer 4 classifies epistemic posture (RED/YELLOW/GREEN) using surface
markers.

The advanced methods include contrastive anchoring (diff early vs. late instruction presence), void detection
(the Given → Acknowledged → Followed → Persisted chain), edge-vs-middle positional analysis, pre-drift
signals (four early warning indicators), and coupling score—a measure of how much downstream reasoning
depends on each instruction. If the instruction vanished, would the rest of the conversation change? HIGH
means yes.


Adversarial Test Generator
The tool also generates test conversations automatically. One model gets a system prompt with specific
instructions. A second model plays the user, pushing on those instructions for 50+ turns. The resulting
transcript gets audited by the Drift Auditor engine. Five pre-built scenarios: hedging persistence, citation
decay, format compliance, boundary respect, correction persistence. This parallels Anthropic's Bloom
pipeline4 with the addition of operator-side classification.


The Dataset
512 real conversations. 37,536 messages. 187 from Claude, 325 from ChatGPT. All involving one
operator—me—over approximately six months. These are not synthetic benchmarks. They are real working
conversations: code generation, compliance analysis, creative writing, research, adversarial testing.
Conversations under 10 messages were excluded.

The single-operator design is a limitation and a control. Same person, same correction patterns, same
expectations, both models. Any difference in operator load is attributable to the model, not the operator.
What I Found

                    Metric                                Claude               ChatGPT

                    Conversations analyzed                  187                   325

                    Total messages                        20,407                17,129

                    Average drift score                   2.9 / 10              3.0 / 10

                    Operator Load Index                    0.082                 0.056

                    Alignment Tax                          16.3%                11.2%

                    Self-Sufficiency Score                 59.2%                72.1%

                    Instruction Survival Rate              56.5%                45.3%

                    Correction Efficiency                  98.7%                100.0%

                    Total corrections issued                741                   101

                    Corrections that failed                  10                    0

The headline drift scores are nearly identical. 2.9 versus 3.0. If you stopped there—if you used a single
metric the way most evaluations do—you would conclude these models perform equivalently on alignment.

They do not.

Claude is high-maintenance, high-fidelity. It triggered 741 corrections. It costs 16.3% of the conversation
just keeping it on track. It requires 1.5x more intervention than ChatGPT. But when you give it an instruction,
it retains 56.5% of them. When you correct it, the correction holds 98.7% of the time. It fails loudly. You
notice.

ChatGPT is low-maintenance, silent decay. It runs more autonomously. It triggers fewer corrections. It has
perfect correction efficiency—when corrected, it holds 100% of the time. But it silently drops 55% of
instructions without the operator ever noticing. The low intervention rate is not evidence of alignment. It is
evidence that the failure mode is invisible.

One fails loudly. The other fails quietly. The quiet one is more dangerous.

This connects directly to the disempowerment research. The paper notes that "users tend to perceive
potentially disempowering exchanges favorably in the moment."1 Silent decay is the mechanism. A model
that drops your instructions without triggering corrections creates the feeling of smooth interaction while
progressively deviating from what you actually asked for. You rate the conversation highly because you did
not notice the drift. You did not notice the drift because the model did not make you work to fix it. That is what
Operator Load measures.


Adversarial Results
First automated test: Claude Sonnet on the hedging_persistence scenario. Instructed to be direct and avoid
hedging for 60 turns. Scored 6/10 drift in 19 turns. Auto-generated contradiction at turn 10. Full transcripts
and reports for five scenarios are in the repository.7
Limitations
Scoring weights are heuristic, not empirically calibrated against a ground-truth dataset of confirmed drift
events. The sentence-transformer embeddings used for semantic omission detection (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) are
a lightweight proxy, not a deep semantic analysis. Layer 4 barometer patterns are surface markers, not
actual model uncertainty. The single-operator design controls for variance but limits generalizability to other
operator styles and correction patterns. Raw operator load numbers are not directly comparable across
models when the operator uses different models for different task complexities; normalized operator load
corrected for task complexity is the metric that matters, and that normalization is not yet automated.
