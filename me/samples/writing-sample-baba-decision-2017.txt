DECISION AND ORDER
DENYING COMPENSATION AND BENEFITS

This claim arises under the Longshore and Harbor Workers‟ Compensation Act, 33 U.S.C.
§§ 901-950, as extended by the Defense Base Act, 42 U.S.C. §§ 1651-1655 (collectively “the Act”).
The parties submitted the matter for decision on the record without a formal hearing. Attorney
David Barnett represented Robert Baba (“Claimant”). Attorney Raymond Warns represented SOS
International, LLC and Allied World Assurance Co. (collectively “Employer”).
The following exhibits were admitted into evidence: Claimant‟s exhibits (“CX”) 1 through
23, including the supplemental pages to CX 15 submitted on August 16, 2016, as well as the
transcript with exhibits for CX 20, submitted the same day, and Employer‟s exhibits (“EX”) 1
through 7. Claimant and Employer each filed closing briefs on September 19, 2016, marked
respectively as Administrative Law Judge Exhibit (“ALJX”) 1 and 2. Claimant and Employer filed
permissible reply briefs, marked respectively as ALJX 3 and 4, on October 3, 2016, thereby closing
the record.

As part of the psychological evaluation, Dr. Meisner administered the following tests:
MMPI-2-RF, PTSD Checklist-Civilian Version, Beck Anxiety Inventory, Best Depression
Inventory-II, and Word Memory Test. EX 5 at 67. According to Dr. Meisner, “the MMPI-2 is the
best researched method for discriminating between exaggerated reporting of symptoms, and the
reports of psychiatric patients who have no incentive to exaggerate.” EX 5 at 70. Based on the
results of the MMPI-2-RF test, which is the latest iteration of MMPI-2 test, Dr. Meisner opined that
Claimant over reported his psychiatric symptoms and cognitive impairment and exaggerated his
somatic distress. EX 5 at 67-68, 70. Dr. Meisner added that there was no evidence of
underreporting of symptoms by Claimant and it was highly unlikely that Claimant‟s test results could
be produced without exaggeration. EX 5 at 68, 70. On the PTSD Checklist-Civilian Version,
Claimant reported a wide range of symptoms of PTSD, some bordering on the extreme. EX 5 at
68. However, Dr. Meisner stated that the results of the test could not distinguish between an
extreme PTSD and exaggerated reporting. Id. Claimant reported extreme levels of anxiety on the
Beck Anxiety Inventory test, yielding a score of 62, which Dr. Meisner said depicted “either an
extreme level of anxiety, exaggeration or some combination of the two.” EX 5 at 68. Similarly,
Claimant reported high levels of depression on the Beck Depression Inventory-II test, producing a
score of 46. Id. Dr. Meisner noted that Claimant‟s clinical appearance did not approximate severe
depression and the results depict “either an extraordinary level of depression, exaggeration or some
combination of the two.” EX 5 at 68, 70. Dr. Meisner said the Word Memory Test result “showed
unequivocal evidence of gross exaggeration of impaired memory.” EX 5 at 68. Dr. Meisner noted
that Claimant relied on his memory to avoid correct responses and suggested that “[a]n individual
who had never seen the test stimuli would have performed far better by guessing.” Id.
24.
Dr. Meisner noted some credibility concerns in his report, pointing to several
inconsistencies in Claimant‟s account of events. EX 5 at 69. For example, Claimant reported to
have lost interest in socializing but later noted that he unexpectedly ran into a friend at a private club
dinner meeting. Id. In addition, Claimant‟s phone, which he placed on the table, sounded on
multiple occasions during the interview and he frequently looked at the display. EX 5 at 51. Dr.
Meisner also noted that Claimant‟s description of his knee and lower back condition seemed to be
exaggerated but deferred to the opinion of relevant medical expert. Id. Dr. Meisner also mentioned
an inconsistency between Claimant‟s appearance and his discussion of his appearance. EX 5 at 70.
According to Dr. Meisner, Claimant reported that he tends not to get a haircut unless prompted by
his mother but was “meticulously groomed” for the examination and arrived wearing a suit and a tie.
Id. Dr. Meisner wrote that it was hard to believe that a person with a disregard for his appearance
would decide to be so meticulously groomed and wear a suit. Id.
25.
Dr. Meisner opined that Claimant had “grossly exaggerated symptoms, impairment
and the severity of traumatic exposure,” and that Claimant‟s vague responses and inconsistencies
also suggested that he had concealed relevant history. EX 5 at 69. Dr. Meisner added that neither
Claimant‟s report nor his behavior could be considered evidence of a mental disorder. Id. Dr.
Meisner concluded that there was no substantial evidence that Claimant suffered from a mental
disorder and does not require restrictions or treatment. Id.
26.
Dr. Meisner added, however, that Claimant‟s “pervasive exaggeration does not rule
out mental disorder” because sometimes individuals who exaggerate also suffer from a mental
disorder. EX 5 at 72. Before concluding that someone who is exaggerating their symptoms also

-8-

suffers from a mental disorder, it is necessary to demonstrate that the purported evidence of mental
disorder is free from exaggerated reporting or exaggerated behavior under the patient‟s voluntary
control. Id. Dr. Meisner said that his observations of Claimant‟s behavior during the examination
did not provide him with any compelling indications of mental disorder.13 Id. Claimant did not
show signs of slowing in motor activities and showed only mild agitation which Dr. Meisner stated
could have been the product of exaggeration. EX 5 at 73. The rate, volume, and flow of Claimant‟s
speech were within normal limits. Id. Claimant did raise his voice once in the course of the
examination, which Dr. Meisner attributed to voluntary behavior, Claimant‟s mood did not appear
depressed, and he showed a normal range of emotional expression. Id. Dr. Meisner also mentioned
that Claimant became tearful when describing his traumatic exposures in Iraq, but there was
something abnormal about the tears, which Dr. Meisner suggested could have been produced with
voluntary contribution. Id.
27.
Dr. Meisner further stated that Claimant‟s history provides reasons to doubt that he
suffers from a mental disorder. EX 5 at 73. Claimant‟s most traumatic exposures occurred during
the early years of his employment, but, after 2011, Claimant did not even work outside the wire. Id.
Dr. Meisner pointed out that Claimant worked as a linguist for six years and filed the claim only
after his high paying work as an interpreter became unavailable. EX 5 at 73, 75. Moreover, after
five years of employment in Iraq, Mr. Marsh, who treated Claimant‟s knee injury in Iraq, reported
that Claimant denied having anxiety and depression and appeared to have normal mood and
emotion. EX 5 at 74. In addition, Claimant‟s symptoms appeared to have developed in the last
three months of his employment, which Dr. Meisner found hard to believe because Claimant did
not witness anyone being injured or killed during his last year of employment with Employer. Id.
28.
Dr. Meisner also opined that there were significant post-employment factors and
events that were much more likely to account for Claimant‟s symptoms rather than a delayed onset
of PTSD. Dr. Meisner said the most obvious was Claimant‟s post-employment adaptation to his
new life after spending six years in Iraq. EX 5 at 77. According to Dr. Meisner, “claimant must
adapt to significant unwanted life changes that are independent of traumatic exposure…the job that
he enjoyed in Iraq for six years was no longer available to him.” Id. In addition, Claimant noted
how much he valued the esteem that he received from working with the military and the pride that
came with successfully operating a business. Id. Dr. Meisner opined that Claimant‟s current
lifestyle and the pursuit of this claim may not meet his esteem needs, and puts Claimant in an
unfavorable light in his family and community. Id. Finally, Claimant was facing the transition to life
after the age of 65. Id. Dr. Meisner concluded that if evidence of mental disorder somehow arose,
then non-industrial factors could account for it more plausibly than exposure in Iraq. EX 5 at 79.
29.
In addition, Dr. Meisner added that “to the extent that there was traumatic exposure,
it occurred in the earliest years of his employment, but the patient was free of re-experiencing
symptoms for years after that.” EX 5 at 76. While working for Employer, Claimant did not work

ANALYSIS

As set forth below, the following conclusions of law are based upon analysis of the entire
record, arguments of the parties, and applicable regulations, statutes, and case law. 29 C.F.R. §
18.92. In deciding this matter, the administrative law judge (“ALJ”) is entitled to weigh the
evidence, to draw inferences from it, and to assess the credibility of witnesses. See Cardillo v. Liberty
Mut. Ins. Co., 330 U.S. 469, 477 (1947); 29 C.F.R. § 18.12. The Act is construed liberally in favor of
injured employees. Voris v. Eikel, 346 U.S. 328, 333 (1953); J.B. Vozzolo, Inc. v. Britton, 377 F.2d 144,
147 (D.C. Cir. 1967). However, the proponent of a rule or position has the burden of proof and,
thus, the burden of persuasion. Dir., OWCP v. Greenwich Collieries, 512 U.S. 267, 275-76 (1994).

A.

Credibility Determinations

In arriving at a decision, the finder of fact is entitled to determine the credibility of witnesses,
weigh the evidence, and draw inferences from the evidence, and is not bound to accept the opinion
or theory of any particular medical examiner or other expert witness. Banks v. Chi. Grain Trimmers
Ass’n, 390 U.S. 459, 467 (1968); Duhagon v. Metro. Stevedore Co., 31 BRBS 98, 101 (1997), aff’d, 169
F.3d 615 (9th Cir. 1999). The credibility of witnesses “involves more than demeanor. It apprehends
the over-all evaluation of testimony in the light of its rationality or internal consistency and the
manner in which it hangs together with other evidence.” Carbo v. United States, 314 F.2d 718, 749
(9th Cir. 1963). An ALJ is not bound to believe or disbelieve the entirety of a witness‟ testimony,
but may choose to believe only certain portions of the testimony. Altemose Constr. Co. v. NLRB, 514
F.2d 8, 14 n.5 (3d Cir. 1975).
In reviewing the medical opinions of record, several considerations are relevant to the ALJ‟s
determination of their probative value and relative evidentiary weight. An ALJ must examine the
logic of a physician‟s conclusions and the evidence upon which those conclusions are based, and
evaluate the physician‟s opinion in light of the other evidence in the record. Newport News
Shipbuilding & Dry Dock Co. v. Ward, 326 F.3d 434, 441-42 & n.4 (4th Cir. 2003); Jackson v. Ceres
Marine Terminals, Inc., 48 BRBS 71, 75 (2014). The judge need not accept the opinion of any
particular medical examiner, particularly if rational inferences urge a contrary conclusion. Pimpinella
v. Universal Maritime Serv., Inc., 27 BRBS 154, 157 (1993); Todd Shipyards Corp. v. Donovan, 300 F.2d 741
(5th Cir. 1962). In evaluating expert testimony, the judge may rely on common sense. Avondale
Indus., Inc. v. Dir., OWCP, 977 F.2d 186, 189 (5th Cir. 1992). A medical opinion that is based on
hypothetical possibilities or generalities, rather than on the evidence specific to claimant, may be
discounted. Cotton v. Army & Air Force Exchange Servs., 34 BRBS 88 (2000). The judge, furthermore,
may base one finding on a physician‟s opinion and on another issue find contrary to the same
physician‟s opinion. Pimpinella, 27 BRBS at 157.
An ALJ is not obligated to rule in favor of a party simply because his or her medical experts
are more numerous or more highly trained. Avondale Shipyards, Inc. v. Kennel, 914 F.2d 88, 91 (5th Cir.
1990), abrogated on other grounds. However, an ALJ may consider a physician‟s credentials and
specialized expertise (or lack thereof) in determining the opinion‟s evidentiary weight. See generally
Monta v. Navy Exchange Serv. Command, 39 BRBS 104, 107-108 (2005). The opinion of a treating
physician generally deserves greater weight than that of a non-treating physician, because the treating
physician “is employed to cure and has a greater opportunity to know and observe the patient as an
individual.” Amos v. Dir., OWCP, 153 F.3d 1051, 1054 (9th Cir. 1998); see also Black & Decker
Disability Plan v. Nord, 538 U.S. 822, 830 n.3 (2003) (rule similar to the Social Security treating
physicians rule, affording such physicians special deference). But, a treating physician‟s testimony is
not automatically entitled to greater weight when the issue is outside the course of medical treatment
to be followed. Duhagon, 31 BRBS at 100-01. A judge also “need not accept a treating physician‟s
opinion which is brief and conclusory in form with little in the way of clinical findings to support
[its] conclusion.” Magallanes v. Bowen, 881 F.2d 747, 751 (9th Cir. 1989) (internal citations omitted).

B.

Legal Analysis
1.

Claimant‟s prima facie case and Section 20(a) presumption

Under Section 20(a) of the Act, an ALJ may presume that a claimant‟s injury causally relates
to employment. 33 U.S.C. § 920(a); Pedroza v. BRB, 624 F.3d 926, 931 (9th Cir. 2010). To invoke
the Section 20(a) presumption, a claimant must establish: (1) that physical harm or pain occurred
and (2) that working conditions existed or an accident occurred that could have led to such harm.
Ramey v. Stevedoring Servs. of Am., 134 F.3d 954, 959 (9th Cir. 1998); Susoeff v. S.F. Stevedoring Co., 19
BRBS 149, 151 (1986). In presenting a prima facie case, the claimant is not required to introduce
affirmative evidence that the working conditions in fact caused the harm; rather, claimant must
merely show that working conditions existed which could have caused the harm. O’Kelley v. Dep’t of
the Army, 34 BRBS 39, 40 (2000). The claimant only needs to show “some evidence” of each
element to invoke the Section 20(a) presumption, and need not establish the elements by a
preponderance of the evidence. Albina Engine & Machine v. Dir., OWCP, 627 F.3d 1293, 1298 (9th
Cir. 2010) (citations omitted). Where a work-related injury or condition aggravates, accelerates or
combines with a pre-existing condition, the entire resulting disability is compensable. See, e.g.,
Strachan Shipping Co. v. Nash, 782 F.2d 513 (5th Cir. 1986) (en banc); Indep. Stevedore Co. v. O’Leary, 357
F.2d 812 (9th Cir. 1966). A claimant‟s credible complaints of subjective symptoms and pain can be
sufficient proof of physical injury and a claimant need not establish any particular diagnoses, just
that something has gone wrong with the human frame. Perry v. Carolina Shipping Co., 20 BRBS 90, 92
(1987); Golden v. Eller & Co., 8 BRBS 846, 849 (1978), aff’d, 620 F.2d 71 (5th Cir. 1980).

- 18 -

Claimant has established a prima facie case to invoke the Section 20(a) presumption. Claimant
worked as an interpreter for military personnel in Iraq for seven years. F.F. ¶ 2. He went on
missions outside the wire alongside the military, and was frequently exposed to artillery fire and
other hazards. F.F. ¶ ¶ 2, 4, 11. While employed by Employer, Claimant witnessed explosions and
gun fire at the military base he where he worked, which also contained an Iraqi prison and
insurgents repeatedly attempted to break out the prisoners. Id. He also reported to Dr. Moghaddam
that he has short term memory loss, insomnia, depression, and anxiety related to his work in Iraq.
F.F. ¶ 16. Based on Claimant‟s history of the injury and subjective complaints, Dr. Moghaddam
diagnosed Claimant with PTSD. Id. There is little doubt that the warlike conditions witnessed by
Claimant could have caused the alleged psychological injury. Based on the aforementioned, I find
the evidence sufficient to invoke the Section 20(a) presumption.
2.

Employer‟s rebuttal of the presumption

If a claimant successfully invokes the Section 20(a) presumption, an employer must present
“substantial evidence” to rebut claimant‟s prima facie case. Haw. Stevedores, Inc. v. Ogawa, 608 F.3d 642,
651 (9th Cir. 2010); see generally Del Vecchio v. Bowers, 296 U.S. 280, 286 (1935). Substantial evidence
must go beyond mere allegations and unreliable speculation and must be more than the submission
of simply any admissible evidence. Rainey v. Dir., OWCP, 517 F.3d 632, 637 (2d Cir. 2008). It must
be “„specific and comprehensive enough to sever the potential connection between the disability and
the work environment.‟” Ogawa, 608 F.3d at 651 (quoting Ramey, 134 F.3d at 959). However, the
evidence need not necessarily support a ruling in an employer‟s favor; instead, it need only be of the
type that “a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Am. Grain
Trimmers, Inc. v. OWCP, 181 F.3d 810, 818 (7th Cir. 1999) (quoting Del Vecchio, 296 U.S. at 286);
accord Rainey, 517 F.3d at 637. An employer may use negative evidence to rebut the presumption if
the evidence is sufficiently specific. Holmes v. Universal Maritime Serv. Corp., 29 BRBS 18, 21 (1995);
Swinton v. J. Frank Kelly, Inc., 554 F.2d 1075, 1083 (D.C. Cir. 1976), cert. denied, 429 U.S. 820 (1976).
Additionally, negative evidence which supplements “positive” medical evidence and a credibility
determination adverse to claimant is also sufficient to rebut the presumption. Holmes, 29 BRBS at
22; see also Mock v. Newport News Shipbuilding & Dry Dock Co., 14 BRBS 275, 279-80 (1981) (negative
evidence may be used to establish alleged accident did not occur).
The determination of whether the employer has produced sufficient evidence to rebut the
presumption is a legal one, and does not involve the weighing of the evidence itself or the credibility
of witnesses. Ogawa, 608 F.3d at 651. Once the presumption applies, employer must also offer
substantial evidence that a claimant‟s condition was neither caused by his working conditions nor
aggravated, accelerated, or rendered symptomatic by such conditions. Id. In addition, “the
unequivocal testimony of a physician that no relationship exists between an injury and a Claimant‟s
employment is sufficient to rebut the presumption.” Holmes, 29 BRBS at 20 (citing Kier v. Bethlehem
Steel Corp., 16 BRBS 128, 130 (1984)); accord O’Kelley, 34 BRBS at 42. The physician need not offer
an alternate theory of causation for the injury in order to rebut the Section 20(a) presumption.
O’Kelley, 34 BRBS at 41; Kier, 16 BRBS at 129.
Here, Dr. Meisner‟s medical report and opinion is sufficient to rebut the presumption of
coverage under the Act. Dr. Meisner offered a compelling opinion, based upon a reasoned and
considered review of the evidence that Claimant grossly exaggerated his symptoms, impairment and

- 19 -

the severity of his traumatic exposure in Iraq. Based upon the results of the psychological tests and
clinical observations, Dr. Meisner opined that neither Claimant‟s reporting of symptoms nor his
voluntary behavior could be considered evidence of a mental disorder. Id. Dr. Meisner said that if
Claimant had a mental disorder, it would be highly unlikely that a work related condition caused it.
Instead, it would be more likely that his adaptation to significant unwanted life changes upon his
return from Iraq caused his condition. F.F. ¶ 33. Dr. Meisner thought because the job that
Claimant enjoyed in Iraq for six years was no longer available to him, the pride he had as a
successful business owner and esteem from working with the military, and the pursuit of this claim
which leaves him unable to work and puts him in an unfavorable light with his family and
community, were all more likely to account for any delayed onset mental disorder symptoms or
PTSD. Id. Further, Dr. Meisner opined that if a psychological injury was found, then it was not due
to Claimant‟s earlier employment because Claimant did not go on missions outside the wire and
denied witnessing any significant injuries in his last year of employment with employer. F.F. ¶ 34.
Dr. Meisner concluded that “to the extent that there was traumatic exposure, it occurred in the
earliest years of his employment.” Id. I find the conclusion of Dr. Meisner that Claimant does not
suffer from a mental disorder to be substantial evidence sufficient to rebut the Section 20(a)
presumption.
3.

Evaluation of the evidence

If an employer is able to introduce substantial evidence to rebut the 20(a) presumption, the
presumption drops from the case and the ALJ must weigh all of the evidence and resolve the issue
of causation based on the record as a whole. Holmes, 29 BRBS at 20; Hughes v. Bethlehem Steel Corp.,
17 BRBS 153, 155 (1985). In such case, the burden shifts to the claimant, who must prove by a
preponderance of the evidence that the alleged injury is work-related. Greenwich Collieries, 512 U.S. at
281; Albina, 627 F.3d at 1298. If the judge finds the evidence evenly balanced, the claim fails.
Greenwich Collieries, 512 U.S. at 281. In weighing the evidence, the ALJ is responsible for determining
credibility and resolving conflicts in medical testimony. Magallanes, 881 F.2d at 750 (citing Allen v.
Heckler, 749 F.2d 577, 579 (9th Cir. 1984)).
Since the Section 20(a) presumption has been rebutted, I must weigh all of the evidence and
resolve the causation issue based on the record as a whole. There are conflicting medical opinions
about whether Claimant sustained a compensable psychological injury arising from the course of his
employment with Employer. Claimant argues that he sustained a psychological injury as a result of
the cumulative effect of what he witnessed and experienced during the course of his employment
with Employer in Iraq and that there was no single causative event that caused the injury. F.F. ¶ 49.
Employer counters that post-employment factors are more likely to have caused Claimant‟s alleged
mental disorder. F.F. ¶ 33. In addition, Employer argues that to the extent that there was traumatic
exposure, it occurred in the earliest years of his employment, when Claimant was not employed by
Employer. F.F. ¶ 34. Lastly, Employer argues that Claimant does not have a mental disorder based
on the results of the psychological tests and clinical observations which showed Claimant was
malingering. F.F. ¶ 30.
There are three opinions offered about PTSD in this case, but only the opinions of Dr.
Meisner and Dr. Friedman have value related to the analysis the mental health diagnosis. The record
also contains Dr. Moghaddam‟s report, who was Claimant‟s treating doctor, in which he diagnosed
Claimant with PTSD. F.F. ¶ 16. Dr. Moghaddam submitted a brief four page report that

- 20 -

summarized his findings based on Claimant‟s office visit on November 25, 2014. F.F. ¶ 16. Dr.
Moghaddam did not conduct psychological tests or note any meaningful clinical observations in his
report that would support or refute a mental disorder or PTSD, but simply noted his diagnostic
impressions based on Claimant‟s history of the injury and subjective complaints. Employer asked
the court to take official notice of the fact that Dr. Moghaddam is not a psychologist or psychiatrist,
and therefore not qualified to render a PTSD diagnosis. ALJX 2 at 13. I decline to take official
notice of Dr. Moghaddam‟s qualifications or his profession as it is not outcome determinative. I
find that there is no evidence to indicate that Dr. Moghaddam has the expertise to render a mental
health diagnosis or PTSD based upon clinical observations, and I find his opinion about PTSD to
have no weight. The two opinions that matter for the purposes of evaluating whether a
compensable psychological injury exists are those of Dr. Friedman and Dr. Meisner.
The issue of the existence of Claimant‟s psychological injury centers on the conflicting
opinions of Dr. Friedman and Dr. Meisner, who reached diametrically opposed conclusions based
on the results of psychological testing, clinical observations, and Claimant‟s self-reporting of
symptoms, traumatic exposures, and past history. Claimant relies on the medical opinion of Dr.
Friedman, who diagnosed Claimant with chronic PTSD based on Claimant‟s history, the results of
psychological testing and his manner of presentation. F.F. ¶ 45. In addition, given the concerns of
Claimant‟s exaggeration of symptoms, Dr. Friedman also relied on medical records, past history, and
the nature of Claimant‟s traumatic exposure. F.F. ¶ 47. In doing so, Dr. Friedman heavily relied on
Claimant‟s self-reporting of symptoms and his accounts of traumatic experiences and appeared to
significantly downplay the results of the objective psychological tests with validity scales. While Dr.
Friedman is a well-qualified medical expert, I find his opinions and conclusions regarding Claimant‟s
psychological injury to be less persuasive and less credible than those of Dr. Meisner.
Dr. Friedman‟s reliance on the results of the psychological testing in reaching his PTSD
diagnoses is questionable and lacks validity. After Claimant‟s questionable performance on the
TOMM, Dr. Friedman noted that Claimant “was either wholly inattentive or was intentionally
providing incorrect answers in an attempt to exaggerate claims of cognitive impairment.” F.F. ¶ 40.
Claimant‟s performance on the TOMM, given the nature of the test, is highly suggestive of
malingering. Dr. Friedman concluded that Claimant consciously and intentionally gave erroneous
responses in attempt to dramatize his claimed cognitive difficulties. More importantly, Dr.
Friedman opined that Claimant‟s performance on TOMM calls into question the validity of all the
tests, which may represent exaggeration of genuine distress. Id. Overstatement and inconsistent
reporting was also suggested by Claimant‟s results on the MMPI-2 test. F.F. ¶ 41. Dr. Friedman
reported that Claimant‟s score “suggested a pattern of overstatement due to numerous rare
complaints” and noted that Claimant‟s “performance on MMPI-2 was of highly questionable
validity.” Id. On the Symptom Checklist-90-R test, which Claimant self-reported his symptoms to
show greater than moderate distress; Dr. Friedman interpreted the results by stating that
“[s]ignificant overstatement was evident on the SCL-90-R.” F.F. ¶ 43. However, Dr. Friedman
determined that Claimant had valid results indicative of a possible mental disorder based on the
Beck Depression and Anxiety Inventory tests, the Epworth Sleepiness Scale test, the Pain Patient
Profile (“P3”) test, and the Rotter Incomplete Sentences Blank test. F.F. ¶¶ 44, 45.
Even though Dr. Friedman obtained a mixed bag of test results, some indicating
exaggeration of symptoms while others generating results within normal ranges, Dr. Friedman
nevertheless diagnosed Claimant with chronic PTSD, stating simply that the exaggeration of
symptoms was a “cry for help.” F.F. ¶ 45. He noted that sometimes patients exaggerate symptoms
- 21 -

in an attempt to impress upon the doctor the severity of their complaints. Id. I find Dr. Friedman‟s
explanation troubling and without sufficient support in the record. Dr. Friedman‟s cursory
justification that the exaggeration of symptoms is a “cry for help,” is not persuasive, as he does not
sufficiently explain how he reached this conclusion. The “cry for help” justification is used
selectively to explain the pattern of exaggeration which otherwise should be a red flag that the
results cannot be trusted. Dr. Friedman did not offer a satisfactory explanation for why the results
should be discounted so significantly. Moreover, Dr. Friedman did not explain why he gave the
tests at all if the results appear to be irrelevant to his findings. It is possible that Claimant was
intentionally giving erroneous responses or exaggerating symptoms even on the tests that showed
results within valid ranges, such as the P3 test, Beck Depression and Anxiety Inventories, and the
Rotter Incomplete Sentence test. I find Dr. Friedman‟s explanation for Claimant‟s exaggerated
symptom reporting to be unpersuasive and detract from Dr. Friedman‟s opinions and conclusions
that rely upon the results of the psychological tests.
Dr. Friedman also heavily relied on Claimant‟s subjective reporting of his traumatic
exposure. Dr. Friedman opined that when there is evidence of exaggeration in psychological testing,
one must look at other factors, such as Claimant‟s traumatic exposures and his life history. F.F. ¶
47. Given Claimant‟s exposures to dangerous and warlike conditions such as death and mayhem in
a war zone, Dr. Friedman opined that Claimant‟s reported history must be given great weight and
outweighed any concerns and doubts created by the testing , noting that “if the traumatic history
presented by the applicant is indeed accurate and truthful. It is certainly reasonable that a disabling
posttraumatic stress disorder has developed.” Id. The problem with his conclusion is that there was
strong suggestion in the record that Claimant was not truthful. Dr. Friedman‟s preference to rely on
Claimant‟s subjective reporting of his traumatic exposures over objective psychological tests with
validity scales is concerning in light of strong evidence of Claimants intentional erroneous responses
and apparent proclivity to dramatize impairment and overstate symptoms. F.F. ¶ ¶ 46, 47. Since
Claimant‟s credibility is questionable, so are Dr. Friedman‟s conclusions.
Dr. Meisner offered an opinion that was completely opposite of the opinion offered by Dr.
Friedman. After reviewing Claimant‟s history, medical records, and conducting psychological
testing, Dr. Meisner opined that there is no substantial evidence that Claimant suffers from a work
related mental disorder. F.F. ¶ 30. I find Dr. Meisner‟s opinions and conclusions to be convincing
and persuasive, and more aligned with the evidence in the record. Dr. Meisner relied both on the
results of psychological testing and his clinical observations and that neither should be considered as
evidence of a mental disorder because the results of medical tests showed that Claimant had “grossly
exaggerated symptoms, impairment and the severity of traumatic exposure.” F.F. ¶ 30. Dr. Meisner
added that Claimant‟s vague responses and inconsistencies also suggested that he had concealed
relevant history. Id.
Dr. Meisner had administered several psychological tests, such as the MMPI-2-RF, that were
also administered by Dr. Friedman, and there were some tests that generated similar results. For
example, based on the results of the MMPI-2-RF test, Dr. Meisner opined that Claimant over
reported his psychiatric symptoms and cognitive impairment, which was similar to the result
obtained by Dr. Friedman on the MMPI-2 test. F.F. ¶ 25, 41. On the Word Memory Test, which
appears to be similar in nature to the TOMM, Claimant showed unequivocal evidence of gross
exaggeration of impaired memory. F.F. ¶ 28. Both doctors were of the opinion that Claimant
intentionally gave erroneous answer. Id.; F.F. ¶ 40. Dr. Meisner‟s clinical observations also
suggested a lack of psychological injury and a propensity to exaggerate symptoms. F.F. ¶ 31.
- 22 -

According to Dr. Meisner, Claimant showed suspiciously dramatic behavior during the interview,
including looking around the room, frequent displays of checking behind his shoulder and even out
the fifth floor window, which Dr. Meisner interpreted as attempts to overstate any psychological
condition. F.F. ¶ 19.
However, despite finding no substantial evidence of a mental disorder, Dr. Meisner did not
entirely rule out a mental disorder. F.F. ¶ 31. Dr. Meisner explained that before concluding that
someone who is exaggerating their symptoms also suffers from a mental disorder, it is necessary to
demonstrate that the purported evidence of mental disorder is free from exaggerated reporting or
exaggerated behavior under the patient‟s voluntary control. Id. Dr. Meisner said that based on
psychological testing and his observations of Claimant‟s behavior, he did not find any compelling
indications of mental disorder. Id. I found Dr. Meisner‟s opinions to be thoughtful, detailed and
well-reasoned, and also better explained in the context of the information available in the record. I
found his opinion to be compelling and entitled to much more weight than the opinion of Dr.
Friedman, who relied too much on subjective information without offering a convincing or even
plausible explanation about the discrepancies with the objective testing information. I was
persuaded by the opinion of Dr. Meisner over Dr. Friedman.
After considering the totality of the evidence, including the medical reports of the medical
experts and the credibility of Claimant, I am not persuaded that Claimant suffers from a workrelated psychological injury. Therefore, I find that Claimant has failed to prove by a preponderance
of the evidence that he suffers from a compensable psychological injury under the Act.
Because I have found Claimant is not entitled to benefits under the Act for PTSD, I do not
reach the additional issues related to AWW, medical benefits and fees and costs.
