FIELD OF THE INVENTION
The present disclosure relates to voice-input architectures, assistive communication interfaces,
speech-recognition pipelines, machine-learning language interfaces, and system-level control
surfaces for mobile and desktop operating systems. More specifically, the disclosure relates to
systems that maintain a persistent assistive control surface, including a floating overlay, bubble,
tray control, native desktop window, or other operating-system-level interface, for capturing
stream-of-consciousness verbal dictation, reconstructing intended meaning from disfluent or
structurally non-standard speech, clarifying uncertain words without disrupting a host
application, and injecting or otherwise delivering resulting text into an active target application
through an operating-system interaction layer.

BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION
1. The Voice-to-Text Commodity Problem
Modern automated speech-recognition (ASR) layers can produce accurate phoneme-to-token
transcriptions for fluent speech. However, ordinary dictation systems remain oriented around
literal transcription. When a user speaks in fragments, self-corrections, code-switches, partial
words, or non-linear stream-of-consciousness structures, these systems often produce a transcript
that is technically close to the acoustic signal but professionally unusable as a message, email,
command, or document insertion.

2. The Clinical Disfluency and Cognitive-Load Gap
Existing voice-input interfaces commonly treat speech disfluencies, including part-word
repetitions, prolongations, severe silent speech blocks, filler words, false starts, and
conversational self-corrections, as noise or transcription errors. For a speaker managing a stutter
or other speech block, ordinary single-pass ASR can preserve the least useful part of the
utterance: the surface-level breakdown rather than the intended message. A speaker who stutters
may also maintain alternate lexical candidates, sentence-repair plans, and semantic intent buffers
while speaking. Requiring that speaker to visually reacquire moving application controls after
dictation imposes additional cognitive load at the exact moment the speaker is preserving a
fragile semantic plan.

3. The Application-Context Invariance Problem
Standard mobile and desktop voice assistants, input-method editors, and dictation surfaces can
disrupt the host application environment during data entry. They may alter window focus, modify
layout insets, invoke a keyboard, change cursor or selection state, force a full-viewport refresh,
or require a separate host application activity. In cross-application communication scenarios,
such disruption forces the user to reorient within the third-party interface, increasing dual-task
cognitive load and reducing practical usefulness.

4. The Multilingual and Interlanguage Reconstruction Problem
Many users communicate across multiple languages or speak a target language through the
structure of a first or heritage language. A transcript may contain recognizable target-language
words while carrying non-native grammar-transfer patterns, such as omitted articles, simplified
tense, missing auxiliaries, literal idiom translation, or transferred word order. Such patterns are
not necessarily acoustic accents. They can be detected from the text after transcription. Ordinary
grammar checkers and dictation systems generally do not combine speech-recognition
uncertainty, user-specific vocabulary, cross-language transfer profiles, and real-time application-
context preservation in one operating-layer communication system.

5. Assistive Target Stabilization Problem
Even after text is reconstructed, the user must often locate and press a native send, microphone,
paste, or commit control. Those controls move across applications, shift when keyboards appear,
and vary by design system. For users with stuttering, motor impairments, executive-function
constraints, high stress, or multitasking load, the post-dictation search for a target control is itself
a failure point. A useful assistive system should preserve the user’s motor target expectations
while retaining final user authorization for any transmission event.

6. Adjacent Technology Landscape
The following adjacent technologies are technically relevant background and are identified to
frame the problem space, not to state a legal conclusion about novelty or validity:
System-level voice assistants such as Siri and Google Assistant can receive speech commands
but generally operate through assistant-specific surfaces, app intents, or host integration models
and do not provide overlay-resident word-level clarification that preserves a third-party text field
state throughout clarification.
Commercial AI dictation tools such as Wispr Flow demonstrate cross-application AI dictation
and floating-button activation in some environments. Such tools are relevant to the general
category of voice-to-text overlays, but the present disclosure focuses on a combination of
bidirectional clarification, context-invariance verification, disfluency-specific reconstruction,
interlanguage-transfer normalization, user-gated target stabilization, and write-integrity recovery.
Keyboard-bound dictation tools such as Gboard voice typing and Apple Dictation provide useful
speech input but are tied to input-method surfaces, keyboard focus, or keyboard layout behavior
and do not provide the disclosed persistent assistive overlay clarification architecture.
Technical disfluency-recognition and ASR research addresses detecting or categorizing disfluent
audio. The present disclosure uses disfluency information as part of an application-control
architecture that includes overlay-resident clarification, text-field manipulation, target
stabilization, and fallback recovery.
Desktop dictation utilities can paste text into an active application, but typically do not combine
clinical stutter-aware reconstruction, per-user trigger modeling, multilingual interlanguage
normalization, visual pipeline-state feedback, and mobile-style target stabilization in a unified
architecture.

BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION
The disclosed system provides a cross-application assistive speech-manipulation architecture
governed by a persistent assistive control surface. In a mobile embodiment, the control surface is
a system-level floating overlay window. In a desktop embodiment, the control surface may be a
native desktop window, dashboard, system tray control, hotkey-controlled interface, or floating
widget paired with operating-system automation. The control surface captures voice input, routes
the input through a layered reconstruction pipeline, presents clarification choices when
uncertainty is detected, and causes the resulting text to be placed into an active host application
while minimizing disturbance to the host application context.
The system shifts the input paradigm from verbatim acoustic translation to intent-driven
semantic reconstruction. A raw or buffered audio stream is processed through one or more ASR
engines and language-model reconstruction layers. The pipeline may incorporate acoustic
confidence, multi-pass decoding disagreement, per-user vocabulary, prior accepted and rejected
reconstructions, clinical disfluency indicators, clock and recipient context, multilingual routing,
and textual interlanguage transfer markers.
During clarification, the system preserves application-context invariance by avoiding host-
application focus changes, cursor movement, scroll movement, input-method-editor state
changes, and selection manipulation. The system may render visual indicators in non-touchable
overlays rather than using host-application selection changes. Authorized text insertion,
replacement, paste, send, or other user-approved operations are treated as controlled output
operations and are separately logged or restored where platform support permits.
The system further provides write-integrity recovery. Where an accessibility, UI automation,
text-write, paste, or simulated-input pathway fails, the controller preserves the reconstructed
payload through a clipboard-marshalling or recovery interface instead of dropping the text. The
recovery path is surfaced to the user as a non-destructive fallback.
The system also provides user-gated target stabilization. After text insertion, the control surface
may enter a send-ready state and reposition or align with the host application’s native send
control. The system does not automatically transmit the message on countdown expiration. A
send event is gated by a separate, intentional user contact or command during the send-ready
state. If the interval expires without user action, the control surface disarms and returns to a
default location without sending.

DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION
1. Core System Architecture
The system comprises an assistive control-surface manager, a speech-capture component, a
speech-recognition layer abstraction, a language-model reconstruction service, a clarifier
component, an operating-system interaction layer, a profile store, and a controller state machine.
The assistive control surface is persistent across application context switches. It may be
implemented as a mobile operating-system overlay window, a desktop native window, a tray-
accessible dashboard, a floating widget, a keyboard/hotkey controlled panel, or any equivalent
control surface that can remain available while the user interacts with a separate host application.
The operating-system interaction layer can include an accessibility framework, UI automation
interface, simulated input pathway, clipboard-marshalling pathway, active-window paste
interface, or other platform-supported mechanism for writing, replacing, pasting, or committing
text in a target application. The controller selects the least disruptive pathway available for the
current host environment and records fallback conditions for later review.

1.1 Mobile Overlay Embodiment and Platform Guardrails
In a mobile embodiment, the assistive control surface is instantiated through the operating
system’s top-level overlay mechanism, such as an Android application overlay window. The
overlay remains visible across host application context switches without taking ordinary window
or input focus from the background active interface. The mobile embodiment may include an
accessibility service that detects focused editable fields, reads non-sensitive contextual text
where permitted, writes reconstructed text into an active field, and dispatches user-approved
gestures to visible controls where permitted by platform policy.
To operate within modern foreground-service and while-in-use microphone constraints, the
controller initiates microphone capture only after a visible user-actuated overlay interaction,
hotword activation in an expressly armed state, or other platform-recognized visible or
foreground condition. The controller binds audio capture to a foreground service lifecycle
configured with appropriate microphone and overlay permissions. Where the platform denies
microphone startup, the controller transitions to a non-recording fallback state and displays a
permission, activation, or troubleshooting guide rather than attempting unauthorized background
capture.

1.2 Desktop Embodiment
In a desktop embodiment, the system is executed as a native or hosted desktop utility. A desktop
process may launch a local voice-reconstruction engine, serve or render a dashboard, expose
profile and calibration controls, register hotkeys such as press-and-hold recording keys, and paste
or insert reconstructed text into the active desktop application. The desktop embodiment may use
a native window wrapper, a local HTTP server, a tray icon, a keyboard-hook listener, a clipboard
pathway, simulated keyboard input, UI Automation, Accessibility APIs, or equivalent operating-
system mechanisms.
A representative desktop implementation includes a voice-reconstruction engine that records
from a microphone, transcribes with an ASR model such as Whisper, optionally executes multi-
temperature voting and confidence targeting, reconstructs with a language model, validates
meaning preservation, and pastes output into the active application. The desktop dashboard may
display session metrics, words per minute, fluency indicators, difficulty scores, profile controls,
stutter-support toggles, delayed auditory feedback settings, vocabulary and correction sets,
trigger words, language settings, accent or interlanguage settings, and data export or deletion
controls.

1.3 Inline Overlay Configuration Interface
The persistent overlay can operate as a standalone runtime configuration surface. A double-tap,
long press, voice command, hotkey, or equivalent user input may expand an inline contextual
configuration payload within the overlay Z-order. The configuration interface can expose
opacity, size, color, theme, language, second-language selection, profile selection, voice
shortcuts, send-ready settings, hotword settings, delayed auditory feedback timing, and assistive
reconstruction-layer controls without requiring the user to open a separate host application view.

1.4 Fluid State-Specific Deformations and Low-Amplitude Idle Kinetics
The assistive control surface may display low-amplitude periodic deformation or other status
animation to indicate readiness. For example, an idle bubble may execute a breathing animation;
a listening state may display a red or enlarged capture state; a processing state may display an
amber state; a verified or send-ready state may display a green state; and a clarifying state may
tilt or split into directional choice regions. These state changes provide low-cognitive-load
feedback without requiring the user to read a dense status panel.
2. Bidirectional Clarification Mechanism
Following speech capture and initial reconstruction, the clarifier component evaluates tokens,
phrases, and intent modes against uncertainty signals. The uncertainty signals may include
acoustic-decoder log probabilities, n-best alternatives, multi-pass decoding disagreement counts,
model-to-model disagreement, phonetic-neighbor matches, user vocabulary matches, historical
correction patterns, prior accepted reconstructions, and semantic confidence estimates.
If a token or phrase falls within a predetermined ambiguity threshold, the controller enters a
CLARIFYING state. The control surface displays a sequential review interface for low-
confidence tokens or phrases. To prevent cognitive fatigue, the system may restrict the display
sequence to no more than three flagged tokens or phrase candidates per utterance cycle.
Throughout the clarification interval, the host application’s window focus, accessibility focus,
input-method-editor state, scroll position, selection state, and cursor position remain unchanged.

2.1 Intent-Ambiguity Clarification Interface
Where the reconstruction service cannot confidently select among intent modes, such as
professional reconstruction, casual reconstruction, direct quotation, command transformation,
translation, or authentic voice preservation, the controller may enter an INTENT_CLARIFYING
state. The assistive surface may display a central query and two or more lateral targets
corresponding to approve, reject, defer, preserve, translate, or alternate reconstruction paths. The
user may select a path by touch, mouse, keyboard, hotkey, gesture, or voice command.

2.2 Word-Level Alternative Sifting Pipeline
When the clarifier isolates a token below the confidence threshold, the controller may enter a
WORD_CLARIFYING state. The control surface displays a small list of replacement candidates
derived from priority matrices, including sentence-boundary continuation predictions, ASR
alternatives, phonetic neighbors, per-user vocabulary, recipient-specific phrase history, language-
family transfer profiles, and prior accepted corrections. A user selection locks in the candidate,
and the controller constructs a revised text segment in which only the flagged token or phrase
differs from the prior target segment. The system then causes the host text field to contain the
revised segment through the available operating-system interaction layer.

3. Transient Coordinate Indicator Overlay
During clarification, a transient visual indicator such as an underline, bracket, glow, or marker
may be rendered over the host application’s text field at screen coordinates corresponding to the
token or phrase under review. In a mobile embodiment, the indicator can be rendered through a
separate non-touchable, non-focusable overlay window so that external finger or pointer contact
passes through to the underlying host controls. Where absolute character bounding boxes are
unavailable, the controller may interpolate the screen coordinates using the text field’s global
layout bounds, approximate character offsets, measured font metrics, or text segment position
data.
4. Text Write and Write-Integrity Recovery Framework
The system attempts to place finalized text into the target field using the least disruptive
supported pathway. On Android, for example, the system may construct a revised full-field string
and invoke a text-write action on an accessibility node. On desktop, the system may use a
clipboard-plus-paste sequence, UI Automation, simulated keyboard entry, or active-window
paste command. The system avoids clipboard use where a direct programmatic write is available,
but preserves a clipboard fallback for failure recovery.
The write-integrity recovery framework evaluates the result returned by the platform action. If
the target node, window, or application refuses the write, exposes no editable node, loses focus,
or returns an execution failure token, the controller preserves the reconstructed text in a local
recovery buffer or clipboard-marshalling handler. The control surface then displays a fallback
guide, such as “tap a text field; your text is preserved,” or a paste-ready state. This prevents loss
of a hard-won utterance where the speaker has navigated a severe speech block or high-stress
cognitive load.

5. Pre-Roll Audio Buffer and Audio-Capture States
The system may maintain a circular pre-roll audio buffer to capture speech immediately
preceding a user’s tap-to-record gesture or hotkey activation. The buffer duration may be fixed or
configurable, for example 500 milliseconds to 3000 milliseconds. Upon activation, the cached
pre-roll audio stream is prepended to the main dictation audio payload before dispatch to the
speech-recognition layer.
To reduce privacy and platform-permission ambiguity, the system may separate audio states. In
an IDLE state, no active microphone capture is occurring. In an ARMED_IDLE state, the user
has enabled a visible armed condition in which low-power keyword spotting or short pre-roll
capture is allowed under platform rules. In a LISTENING state, the primary dictation audio
channel is active. In a POST_CAPTURE or PROCESSING state, the microphone may close
while ASR and reconstruction execute. The controller may display distinct visual states for each
state.

6. Cross-Model Validation Matrix
Before injecting a synthesized prose string into the host interface, the system may execute an
automated validation sequence. A primary reconstruction model generates candidate output from
the raw or cleaned transcription. A second model, validator, rule engine, or independent decoding
pass evaluates the candidate output against the original transcript and any confidence metadata to
isolate omissions, unsupported additions, or meaning drift. Tokens or phrases flagged by the
validator are appended to the clarifier review loop with elevated uncertainty priority. This
validation layer is optional and may be disabled in low-latency modes.

7. Layered Reconstruction Architecture and Onboarding Intent Tuning
The system dynamically adjusts its processing pipeline across plural reconstruction profiles
anchored by an onboarding and usage-history matrix. During initial configuration, the system
may compile user intent data including typical work hours, industry, primary use cases, tone
preferences, native-language status, selected languages, vocabulary, corrections, trigger words,
privacy preferences, and preferred balance between work-style and life-style prose. At runtime,
the controller evaluates live system-clock metadata, foreground application identity, recipient
identity where available, user-selected layer, and prior accepted outputs to choose reconstruction
parameters.

7.1 Layer 1: Verbatim Sub-Pipeline
A first layer optimizes for literal phonetic extraction. It may use a native on-device ASR
subsystem, a cloud ASR endpoint, a local model, or a combination thereof. The system may
apply deterministic disfluency filtering, filler stripping, repetition collapse, punctuation insertion,
or confidence annotation while preserving a generally literal transcript.

7.2 Layer 2: Intent-Driven Reconstruction
A second layer standardizes stream-of-consciousness input by parsing semantic intent variables.
It restructures broken syntax, inserts punctuation, smooths fragmented phrasing, and produces
text that better reflects what the user intended to communicate rather than the literal acoustic
sequence.

7.3 Layer 3: Contextual Personal Adaptation
A third layer augments reconstruction using a per-user lexicon, domain vocabulary, accepted
examples, rejected examples, correction mappings, trigger words, recipient style preferences, and
application-specific history. The profile store may weight entries by recency, acceptance rate,
manual correction frequency, and active context.

7.4 Layer 4: Clinical Disfluency and Environmental Noise Isolation
A fourth layer applies disfluency-aware reconstruction parameters. The system may track part-
word repetitions, prolongations, silent blocks, filler postponements, word substitutions,
circumlocutions, sentence abandonment, content/function word status, sentence position, word
length, phonetic onset, user-specific trigger words, and low-confidence ASR segments. These
signals guide the reconstruction engine to treat parts of the transcript as unreliable surface
artifacts rather than final meaning.
Layer 4 may also isolate environmental or cognitive-load discontinuities. For example, where the
speech stream contains a work message interrupted by passenger-cabin side-talk, the engine may
cross-reference contextual metadata, isolate the non-contextual tangent, collapse repeated
restarts, and synthesize a single coherent professional text string.

7.5 Layer 5: Textual Interlanguage Reconstruction
A fifth layer detects non-native or cross-linguistic grammar-transfer patterns from the transcribed
text stream independent of acoustic accent classification. The layer identifies textual transfer
markers including omitted articles, tense or aspect simplification, preposition substitution, direct
word-order transfer, missing auxiliary verbs, pluralization irregularities, literal idiom translation,
and calque-like sentence structures. The system maps detected markers against a user-specific or
language-family-specific transfer profile and reconstructs the intended message into idiomatic
target-language prose while preserving semantic intent.
The layer may expose a user-selectable mode that normalizes detected patterns toward idiomatic
target-language conventions or preserves them as authentic speaker voice. For example, an input
transcription of “I go to store, I bring you milk” may be reconstructed as “I’m going to the store,
and I’ll bring you some milk.” Because detection operates on transcribed text rather than the
acoustic signal, the layer assists non-native, heritage, bilingual, and elderly speakers without
classifying a speaker by accent, ethnicity, or national origin.

7.6 Multilingual and Code-Switching Reconstruction
The reconstruction engine supports a plurality of natural-language profiles. A language-routing
component detects or receives a selected source language for each utterance, including, by way
of non-limiting example, English, Spanish, German, Mandarin Chinese, Russian, Armenian,
French, Portuguese, Arabic, Hindi, and additional language profiles. For a given utterance, the
controller may route the audio payload to a language-specific ASR model, a multilingual ASR
model, or a language-specific reconstruction prompt.
The system may preserve the source language and perform same-language cleanup, translate the
utterance into a selected target language, or handle intra-utterance code-switching in which an
utterance mixes two or more languages. Selection among these modes may be governed by user
profile, application identity, recipient identity, explicit language selection, automatic language
identification, prior accepted reconstructions, and runtime context.

8. Advanced Disfluency Metrics, Quiet Capture, DAF, and Voice Configuration
8.1 Rate-Gap Disfluency Tracking Engine
The system may measure the immediate syllable-per-minute or word-per-minute generation
velocity of an active vocal segment and compute a variance ratio against a rolling personal
median. Where the immediate generation velocity falls below a target ratio, the system flags an
active speech-block event. The flag may expand token search radiuses, increase reliance on
semantic context, trigger silent-block bridging, or adjust downstream reconstruction prompts.

8.2 Acoustic Silent-Block Bridging
Upon detection of a prolonged acoustic gap occurring after a valid partial phrase has already
been captured, the system may suspend a timeout, route the partial phrase to a reconstruction
engine, and generate sentence-boundary completion candidates. The candidates may appear on
the control surface around a central “use” target. A manual gesture, hotkey, or voice selection
injects or accepts a candidate without requiring the speaker to force vocal production through the
block.

8.3 Dynamic Quiet Mode and Gain Optimization
The audio processing architecture may include a low-amplitude tracking loop that detects
whisper-level or privacy-preserving speech. Upon detection, the controller may apply localized
gain adjustment, invoke a quiet-mode reconstruction profile, and display a low-intensity visual
state indicating that quiet capture is active.

8.4 Delayed Auditory Feedback and Prosodic Signals
The system may include delayed auditory feedback with configurable delay values. It may also
derive paralinguistic or prosodic features, including pause ratio, pitch, energy, speech rate, non-
speech acoustic events, cough, laugh, sigh, breath, and throat-clear markers. These features may
be injected into the reconstruction prompt or profile model to prevent hallucination near non-
speech audio and to preserve intended tone.

8.5 Voice-Triggered Localized Interface Rotation
The control surface may include a voice-triggered configuration mode. Upon registration of a
command phrase, the surface executes a rotation, twist, radial, or other localized animation that
exposes configuration options such as layer selection, opacity, size, language, quiet mode, or
send behavior. The user may navigate and lock options by subsequent voice commands, touch,
pointer movement, or keyboard input.

8.6 Hands-Free Acoustic Summon
The system may include an on-device keyword-spotting loop that monitors for a designated
wake phrase and phonetically proximate variants. Upon a phrase match, the controller transitions
from an armed state to active capture. Where keyword spotting and dictation contend for the
microphone, the controller executes a microphone-arbitration handshake that releases the
keyword recognizer during primary dictation and re-arms it when the system returns to an idle or
armed-idle state.

9. Interface Automation and Dynamic Target Stabilization

9.1 User-Gated Send-Commit Capability
The system includes a user-gated send-commit capability. After text insertion completes, the
control surface may enter a send-ready state by displaying a distinctive affirmative visual state,
such as green, and starting a visible countdown interval. Direct user contact, a pointer click, a
hotkey, or an explicit voice command during the interval authorizes the system to actuate the
host application’s send control. If the interval expires without the user action, the controller
disarms the send pathway and does not transmit the message.
The countdown is a disarming window rather than an automation window. The system does not
dispatch a predictive or automatic send action upon countdown expiration. Final transmission
authorization remains with the user.

9.2 Assistive Target Stabilization Module
To reduce layout-shift errors and preserve motor target expectations, the system may implement
an assistive target stabilization module. When enabled, entering the send-ready state causes the
controller to identify the native send control of the host application through accessibility nodes,
UI automation nodes, resource identifiers, content descriptions, geometric heuristics, or other
platform-supported signals. The control surface then repositions, aligns, or visually anchors itself
to coincide with the native control’s screen bounds or center coordinates.
If the host application layout shifts, such as when an input method editor appears, the control
surface may translate in sequence with the target bounds. Upon successful send, send failure,
user cancellation, or timeout expiration, the control surface returns to a stored pre-alignment
location. The module is assistive: it preserves the user’s learned native-control reach pattern
without removing the final user action gate.

9.3 Input Method Editor Inset Synchronization
To prevent the control surface from being covered by a soft keyboard or input-method editor, the
overlay controller may register layout-inset or window-animation callbacks. When an IME
visibility transition occurs, the controller recalculates the overlay’s coordinate boundaries in
synchronization with the keyboard ascent or descent vector, maintaining accessibility of
clarification targets and send-ready controls.

9.4 Voice-Input Control Alignment
The control surface may also align itself with a host application’s or keyboard’s voice-input
control when that control becomes visible. The controller scans for content descriptions, resource
identifiers, icon markers, or positional heuristics corresponding to a microphone or voice input
asset, then translates the control surface to that location. When the control disappears, the surface
reverts to a user-configured default position.

9.5 Spatial Text-Grab and Transformation Mode
The system may include a spatial text-grab and transformation mode. When a user highlights text
in a source viewport and drags or positions the control surface near the selected text, the
operating-system interaction layer extracts the selected text directly from the accessibility tree,
UI automation tree, selection API, or clipboard-safe pathway. The system routes the selected text
and a spoken transformation directive to the reconstruction engine. The output can replace the
selected text or be injected into a target application after user approval.

9.6 Non-Linear Drag-to-Dismiss Distortion
To minimize visual friction, the control surface may implement a drag-to-dismiss boundary. As
the user drags the surface toward a dismissal region, the controller applies a coordinate-
dependent graphic transformation in which the surface compresses along one axis, elongates
along another, and fades toward transparency. Upon terminal intersection with the dismissal
boundary, the controller tears down the view hierarchy or hides the window.

9.7 Indirect Coordinate Tracking Mode
The window manager may support an indirect coordinate tracking mode in which the control
surface’s center follows the real-time movement vector of a user’s finger, pointer, stylus, or drag
event even when the original gesture is owned by another window or source view. This allows
the surface to be repositioned without saturating the overlay’s own gesture interception
framework.

10. System State Machine Constraints
The controller may operate through a sealed or otherwise constrained state architecture.
Representative states include:
IDLE: no active microphone capture; control surface hidden, visible, or dormant.
ARMED_IDLE: user-enabled visible or foreground condition; low-power keyword spotting or
short pre-roll permitted where platform rules allow.
LISTENING: primary audio channel active; dictation payload recording; control surface displays
capture state.
TRANSCRIBING: audio stream closed or segmented; ASR decoding active.
STRIPPING: deterministic token filtering, disfluency stripping, repetition collapse, or text
normalization executing.
READING_CONTEXT: operating-system interaction layer captures permitted foreground
package, active-window, recipient, layout, or metadata signals.
RECONSTRUCTING: selected reconstruction service parses text against intent, profile,
language, and context parameters.
VALIDATING: optional validation, hallucination-risk grading, or meaning-preservation check
executing.
CLARIFYING: word-level or phrase-level clarification interface active.
INTENT_CLARIFYING: intent-choice interface active.
INJECTED: text-insertion pathway completed or fallback buffer preserved.
SEND_READY: user-gated send target armed; countdown active; optional target stabilization
enabled.
FAILED: non-fatal or fatal error caught; system disarms cleanly and preserves recoverable text
where possible.

10.1 Context-Invariance Integrity Verification
To verify application-context invariance, the controller may execute entry and exit state
snapshots during clarification. Snapshot metrics may include window focus handles, accessibility
focus identifiers, cursor or selection indices, viewport scroll bounds, visible-window identifiers,
input-method-editor state, and anchor nodes. The controller may assert equality across the
clarification interval and maintain an evidentiary record that the clarification interface did not
alter host context.
The invariance assertion is scoped to the clarification interval unless otherwise stated.
Authorized text mutations, user-authorized send gestures, platform-imposed cursor movement
caused by text-write primitives, paste effects, and explicit user actions are recorded separately.
Where supported, the system may restore cursor position or report any post-injection cursor
displacement rather than treating such displacement as a clarification-context change.

11. Pipeline Latency Intermediaries
During transcription and reconstruction, the controller may balance local and remote processing
based on resource availability, network status, layer choice, and user latency preference. Local
decoding may provide privacy and offline resilience; remote decoding may provide higher
accuracy or throughput. The control surface may display progress segments, animation stages, or
console output to mask latency and reduce uncertainty during processing.

12. Predictive Contextual Indicator
Before speech capture begins, the persistent control surface may display a predicted recipient,
medium category, style parameter, language, layer, or difficulty estimate derived from a usage-
history model. The model may be conditioned on foreground application identity, live system-
clock metadata, day-of-week, recipient identifier, prior accepted reconstructions, and profile
settings. The predicted style parameter may bias reconstruction and candidate ordering before the
user begins speaking.

13. Profile Learning, Data Control, and User Corrections
The profile store may maintain vocabulary, corrections, filler words, trigger words, accepted
outputs, rejected outputs, pronunciation or onset weights, interlanguage transfer markers, per-
application preferences, and privacy settings. The user may add or remove words, corrections,
trigger words, and no-flag applications. The system may expose export and deletion controls so
the user can review, back up, or remove profile data. In signed-in embodiments, the profile may
sync across devices; in local embodiments, it may remain device-local.

ABSTRACT OF THE DISCLOSURE
A method and system for cross-application assistive speech reconstruction on a computing
operating system. A persistent assistive control surface, such as a mobile overlay bubble or
desktop dashboard, captures voice input and routes it through layered reconstruction profiles that
convert disfluent, multilingual, non-native, or cognitively fragmented speech into intended text.
A bidirectional clarification interface displays low-confidence tokens or intent choices while
preserving host application focus, cursor, scroll, selection, and input-method state during
clarification. The system inserts or replaces text using an operating-system interaction layer and
preserves the payload through a write-integrity fallback routine when direct insertion fails.
Additional embodiments provide pre-roll capture, silent-block bridging, quiet-mode gain
adjustment, delayed auditory feedback, textual interlanguage reconstruction independent of
acoustic accent classification, multilingual and code-switching routing, and user-gated send-
target stabilization in which final transmission requires an intentional user action and countdown
expiration causes disarming without sending.
